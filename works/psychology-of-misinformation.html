Psychology of misinformation shoots itself in it's leg

Essay · 2026 · 14 min read

For the last decade, the field that calls itself the psychology of misinformation has been very busy and very loud. It has produced studies, dashboards, news cycles, panels at Davos, and a small library of meta-analyses politely concluding that the previous meta-analyses were probably overstating things. It has also, almost without noticing, become the thing it was meant to study.

The argument I want to make here is uncomfortable but, I think, fairly simple: the dominant research program on misinformation has trained itself to find a phenomenon whose definition guarantees the result, and then to recommend interventions whose efficacy is measured against the same definition. It is a closed loop, and from the inside the loop looks like science.

Three moves in particular do most of the damage. First, the operationalisation of "misinformation" as any claim a designated authority has flagged as false — which folds the political question of who counts as authoritative into a measurement instrument. Second, the reliance on stated belief in short surveys as a proxy for the much messier thing we actually mean by believing something. Third, the steady drift from describing what people do with information toward prescribing what they ought to do with it, usually in the imperative voice and usually addressed to someone else.

None of these moves are stupid in isolation. Each of them was, at the time it was made, a pragmatic compromise. Stacked together, they produce a research program that cannot help but flatter its own assumptions, and that grows more confident the less it can be falsified.
